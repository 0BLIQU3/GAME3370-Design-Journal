Mancala

What makes an experience a game? Use a specific moment from Mancala or your mini design challenge to explain how rules, goals, constraints, and feedback shaped what a player did or felt. Describe one change you made—or would make—after observing another player, and connect your reasoning to one idea from the Schell reading. Each weekly post should include at least one visual aid to support your thoughts.

Design

An experience becomes a game when there are not only rules and goals for it, but also a way for a player to exert their will over the game: the ability for a player to feel acknowledged by a game is what allows them to experience enough "fun" to actually consider it a game. When it came to Mancala, I did find merit in how my own actions did have considerable impact on the board state. However, I think my issue with Mancala is that it seems too much like a solved game: the first player is greatly inclined to win, and in the face of near-perfect play, it might feel a bit hopeless for the other player to stake a comeback or "read" a potential move. It felt as though the game was mainly reliant on my own mental calculations more than directly accounting for my opponent. For a simple potential improvement to the game (that might exclusively work for an online environment), being able to tell what pit you will land in with your selection quicker would be good (ex: highlighting the landing pit when you hover over a pit in online Mancala environments).

However, I think to help improve the game on a deeper level though, there needs to be a way to account for that early advantage that the first player can easily push, perhaps a way for player 2 to potentially stake more on the line to turn that tide. I'm not entirely sure that this would be a perfect solution (more firsthand play of Mancala would probably be necessary), but I was thinking that if players had a way to reintroduce more pieces into their own control, then that could allow for a potential comeback with good play. One of the lenses that Schell focuses on is meaningful choice, and one of the concerns about Mancala is that, when you are behind and getting starved of pieces on your own side, your meaningful choices are actively being reduced by the other player. To summarize this idea, it'd be like forgoing your usual turn in exchange for taking some of your banked seeds to put them back into play, which can return agency to you (while not being without its risks, as this effectively allows an opponent to attack your banked seeds). This should effectively help introduce the triangularity that Schell refers to, and it shouldn't be too difficult to account for in actual gameplay since it's a mechanic to potentially benefit a behind player that won't involve constant recalculation of who's in the lead.
