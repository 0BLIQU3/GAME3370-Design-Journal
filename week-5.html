Interest Curve

Consider the interest curve and apply its thought to roguelikes. Analyze how this curve changes over time and what design choices drive those changes. Compare this progression approach with another game and consider ways to balance engagement.

Design

It has appeared to me that many roguelikes build up tension throughout a run only to drop it very sharply at their end when a player is very quickly killed by something they weren't adequately prepared for: I find these deaths flawed in part because of their lack of understandability, as such deaths may be abrupt enough to feel "random". They might also arguably be too punishing: one small mistake at your toughest challenge yet may cost the whole run, and I do believe that this is often considered to an extent in many roguelikes as well. It is not unpopular for games to provide some form of innate "one-shot protection" to offload the weight of that one final mistake, but it's arguable that this problem still extends to "two-shot" or "three-shot" levels of punishment. However, despite being a roguelike, I have found that Cogmind has avoided this issue entirely, maintaining interest at a more "consistently volatile" level that does not "surprise" the player with how a run has failed.

Cogmind is a traditional roguelike, and one where a key philosophy behind its design is to allow for a player to make "comebacks" in gameplay. When mapped to an interest curve, I would consider it to have a great deal of alternating peaks and valleys respectively, in contrast with many other roguelikes today that tend to see a more steady upwards trend of interest. Many modern roguelikes or roguelites tend to have this trend as a run within the game is largely consistent of steadily collecting more and more facets of a goal build to combat increasing challenge, and this run is typically lost upon finally meeting a wall that cannot be surmounted with the current build.

While Cogmind does have an element of putting together pieces of a goal build, I believe that part of what allows it to have such great variety in interest is the fact that builds are not only built up but naturally broken down as well. Items are capable of being shot off of the player and destroyed during combat, so a player can be reduced to a lesser state than previously (prompting more avoidant behavior out of players as they run from threats). To facilitate this, it seems to me that the player character within Cogmind also has a very large pool of health that cannot be restored easily, so unlike many other roguelikes, there is also not a chance of instant death or being "one-shot" out of your prime build. Rather, the interest curve has a generally slower decline over time as players lose a war of attrition, which I believe is what keeps a "comeback" so feasible. At any moment during this decline, a player is capable of finding new items to repair their build, so there is still a relatively high level of interest overall even within these moments.
